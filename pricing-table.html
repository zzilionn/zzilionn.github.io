Case Study

Domain Pricing Table Redesign for a Cloud Computing Service

Product Design Information Architecture Usability
Tools used Figma
Background

A leading European cloud providers domain pricing page is a key conversion point — but user complaints and weak usability signalled it wasn't doing its job.

Problem

Eight columns of jargon-heavy data with no mobile support made it nearly impossible to find the one number that mattered: the first-year price.

Approach

Mobile-first redesign: stripped the table to three essential columns, moved repetitive data out, and introduced search and filter to replace scrolling through hundreds of rows.

Outcome

A scannable, filterable pricing table ready for handoff — scoped in two tiers so the team could ship search first and roll out filters incrementally.

Background Problem Audit Workflow Approach Outcome

Background

A table that does a lot of work.

The domain pricing page sits at the intersection of multiple funnels — linked from the main domain showroom, from CTAs across the site, and pulled in organically through search. It's one of those rare pages that has to work for everyone: first-time buyers, existing customers checking renewal costs, and people who landed there from search engines. The challenge is doing all of that without turning the page into a spreadsheet that only a product manager could love.

Old designThe original table: eight columns, hard-coded in the CMS, assembled without a UX review. Every column competed for equal attention — including ones most users would never need.

Problem

Too much to read, too little to understand.

The table had been built by a product manager and hard-coded in the CMS — and it showed. Eight columns included domain bundles, ICANN fees, and restore fees, most of which required clicking through tooltips to understand. The first-year price — the one number a buyer actually needs — was buried in the same visual weight as renewal and transfer costs. On mobile, things broke entirely: no way to filter, no search, just a horizontal scroll through hundreds of rows. Customer complaints called it out specifically: users didn't know what they'd pay in year one.

Old designOn mobile, the table became a wall of data. No search, no filter, no way to find a specific TLD without scrolling through the entire list alphabetically.

Audit

What's here, what's wrong, what has to go.

Before touching the layout, I worked through three lenses: the companies design system (to understand what the component library could support), the existing table's data model (to decide what to cut, consolidate, or relocate), and stakeholder notes from internal discussions that had already surfaced the core pain points.

The findings were clear. The ICANN fee and restore fee didn't belong as primary columns — they're edge-case information that pulls focus from the buying decision. Domain bundles were dynamic offers the table couldn't reliably maintain. The header colour was out of spec. And the "show all" behaviour, which dumped the full list in alphabetical order, punished users who just wanted to find a specific TLD. The table was structured around the product catalogue, not around how someone actually shops for a domain.

Workflow

Column by column, decision by decision.

Figma

The process started with a column audit: mapping each column to an actual user need and deciding what stays, what moves, and what gets cut entirely. From there, I moved into mobile-first wireframing — building the TLD-as-header pattern that makes rows scannable on small screens, then scaling up to desktop. The filter and search system was scoped in two tiers: an MVP with search only, and a full version with the filter overlay, so the team could ship incrementally. Each interaction decision was documented as an annotated comment directly in the Figma file, following the team's existing handoff convention.

Approach

Mobile first, features earned.

The core move was to strip the table down before building back up. Three columns only: registration, renewal, transfer. The ICANN fee and restore fee are being relocated to a separate secondary section further down the page. Repeating data that cluttered every row without adding value got pulled out entirely.

On mobile, the TLD extension becomes the row header rather than a cell in the first column, which makes the list dramatically easier to scan and sort. Instead of paginating the full list, the table shows the top results with a "show all" nudge — framing it so users are guided toward the filter rather than expected to scroll through hundreds of rows. Search lets anyone find a specific TLD in seconds. The filter overlay covers sorting, offer type and pricing columns shown — designed as a bottom sheet with a clear Reset affordance, keeping the main table uncluttered. The Buy button was spec'd as a configurable anchor or page link to keep the handoff flexible.

RedesignThe filter sheet on mobile. Sorting, offer type, visible columns, and domain type — all accessible without leaving the table view. Return and Reset are always in reach.

RedesignThe filter function in action.

Outcome

Less table, more decision.

The redesign cuts the table from eight columns to three, moves edge-case fees where they belong, and gives users real tools to navigate a long list — search, filter, and a nudge toward filtering rather than scrolling. The mobile experience goes from broken to purposeful. The two-tier scope means search can ship fast while filters follow in the next sprint.

RedesignThe redesigned desktop view. Three columns, inline filters, a search bar — and a "show more" button that nudges users toward filtering rather than scrolling to the bottom.
